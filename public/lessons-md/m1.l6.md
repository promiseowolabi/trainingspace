# M1.L6 — Sampling

_Track M1: The Transformer · ~15 min · trainingspace_

> Training only ever asks the model for a probability distribution over the next token. Generation is what you do with that distribution — and temperature, top-k and top-p are three small edits to it that change the text completely.
## From a loss to a generator

Training minimises cross-entropy at every position: make `p(next token | previous tokens)` assign high probability to what actually came next. Nothing in the loss mentions generating text. But a model that has learned `p(token_t | tokens_<t)` well can produce text by **using its own predictions as input**:

```python
seq = prompt_ids
for _ in range(max_new):
    logits = model(seq[-block_size:])[-1]     # distribution for the NEXT token only
    seq.append(choose(logits))               # greedy or sampled
```

This is **autoregressive** generation. The model was trained on every position in parallel (the causal mask made that honest, M1.L3); it generates one token at a time because each new token depends on the one before.

## Greedy: always take the top token

`choose = argmax`. Deterministic, and usually bad for open-ended text: it falls into loops ("the the the", or the same sentence repeated), because once a repetition is the most likely continuation it stays the most likely. Greedy is fine for short, constrained outputs — a classification label, a single number.

## Temperature

Divide the logits by T before the softmax. For the logits `[3, 2, 1, 0.5, −1]`:

| temperature | probabilities | top token | perplexity |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 0.862, 0.117, 0.016, 0.006, 0.000 | **0.86** | 1.61 |
| 1.0 | 0.624, 0.229, 0.084, 0.051, 0.011 | 0.62 | 2.84 |
| 2.0 | 0.417, 0.253, 0.154, 0.120, 0.056 | 0.42 | 4.12 |

Below 1 the distribution sharpens towards greedy (T → 0 *is* greedy); above 1 it flattens towards uniform. Perplexity here is exp(entropy): "as uncertain as choosing among this many options".

## Truncation: top-k and top-p

A 50,000-token vocabulary has a long tail: thousands of tokens each with tiny probability that together can hold a few percent of the mass. Sample a long text and you will hit the tail eventually, and one absurd token derails everything after it. Truncation removes the tail before sampling:

- **top-k**: keep the k most likely tokens, renormalise. Simple, but k is the same whether the model is confident (where k = 40 keeps junk) or uncertain (where k = 40 cuts real options).
- **top-p (nucleus)**: keep the smallest set of top tokens whose probabilities add up to p (say 0.9), renormalise. The kept set shrinks when the model is confident and grows when it is not.

In the T = 1 row above, top-p 0.8 keeps the first two tokens (0.624 alone is short of 0.8; 0.853 reaches it). Lab 06's `sample_next` implements all three, in the order temperature → top-k → softmax → top-p, and checks the result statistically over thousands of draws.

---

_fig 1 — one decoding step_

Components: logits ((V,)) · ÷ temperature (sharpen/flatten) · top-k (keep k) · softmax (probabilities) · top-p (keep mass p) · draw (append id)

Steps:
1. The model produces one logit per vocabulary entry for the last position. Everything after this is post-processing; the model has finished its work.
2. Dividing by the temperature rescales how far apart the logits are: smaller temperature, larger gaps, a more peaked distribution.
3. Top-k sets every logit outside the k largest to −∞; the softmax then gives them exactly zero probability.
4. Top-p sorts the probabilities and keeps the shortest prefix whose mass reaches p, then renormalises. One random draw picks the token, which is appended to the sequence for the next step.

---

> **[warning]** Temperature and truncation reshape a distribution the model has already computed; they cannot add knowledge. When an evaluation compares two models, fix the decoding settings — or use greedy — or the comparison measures the settings. Likewise, a run's validation **loss** (computed on the full distribution, no sampling) is the clean training signal; sampled text is a sanity check, not a metric.

**Q1. A greedy-decoded story repeats the same sentence forever. What is the most direct fix?**
   A. Train for longer
   B. Sample instead of taking the argmax — e.g. temperature 0.8 with top-p 0.9 — so a repetition that is merely the most likely continuation is not chosen every time
   C. Increase the context length
   D. Use a larger vocabulary
   Answer: B — Greedy decoding always takes the mode, and repetitive continuations often are the mode once a loop starts. Sampling breaks the loop by sometimes choosing other likely tokens. Repetition penalties are another option; more training rarely removes greedy loops.

**Q2. Why is top-p usually preferred to top-k for open-ended text?**
   A. It is faster to compute
   B. Its cut-off adapts to the model's confidence: when one token dominates it keeps very few, when many are plausible it keeps many — a fixed k is too loose in the first case and too tight in the second
   C. It never changes the distribution
   D. It does not need a softmax
   Answer: B — top-k keeps a fixed count regardless of how the probability is spread. top-p keeps a fixed amount of probability mass, so the number of candidates follows the model's uncertainty.

**Q3. A team reports that model A beats model B on a creative-writing evaluation. A was sampled at temperature 0.7, B at 1.2. What should you ask?**
   A. Nothing — temperature does not affect quality
   B. For a comparison at matched decoding settings: different temperatures change outputs systematically, so the result may measure the settings rather than the models
   C. Whether B used top-k
   D. Which tokenizer they used
   Answer: B — Temperature reshapes every distribution: lower is more conservative and coherent, higher more varied and error-prone. Evaluations must fix decoding parameters (or sweep them for both models) before attributing a difference to the weights.

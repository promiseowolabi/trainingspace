# O1.L3 — Optimisers

_Track O1: Networks & Optimisation · ~21 min · trainingspace_

> Plain gradient descent takes one step size for every parameter. On a loss surface that is a thousand times steeper in one direction than another, that step is either unstable or useless — and the fix, per-parameter step sizes, is why every LLM is trained with Adam.
T0.L1's update was `p −= lr · g`: one learning rate for every parameter. Real loss surfaces punish that. Think of a long, narrow valley: very steep across, gently sloped along. A step size small enough not to bounce off the steep walls crawls along the floor.

Make it concrete: the loss `½(1000·x₀² + x₁²)`, starting at (1, 1). Its curvature is 1,000 in one direction and 1 in the other — a **condition number** of 1,000. Gradient descent is stable only if `lr < 2 / 1000`, and at that step size the gentle direction shrinks by at most 0.2% per step. Measured with lab 03's code, 300 steps:

---

- **0.16** — SGD, lr 0.0019 (the steep direction is solved; x₁ is still 0.57 — 300 steps of 0.19% shrinkage)
- **3.4·10⁻⁷** — SGD + momentum 0.9 (same lr; the consistent direction accumulates speed)
- **4.8·10⁻¹⁰** — Adam, lr 0.05 (with 10 warmup steps and cosine decay (lab 03's lr_at))
- **1,000** — condition number (ratio of the steepest to the gentlest curvature)

---

## Momentum: remember the direction

Keep a running sum of past gradients and step along that instead:

```
buf ← μ · buf + g          (μ ≈ 0.9)
p   ← p − lr · buf
```

Across the valley the gradient flips sign every step, so the contributions cancel. Along the floor it points the same way every step, so the buffer grows towards g / (1 − μ) — ten times the raw gradient at μ = 0.9. The oscillation is damped and the slow direction speeds up: 0.16 becomes 3.4 × 10⁻⁷.

## Adam: a step size per parameter

Adam keeps two running averages per parameter: of the gradient (**m**, momentum) and of the squared gradient (**v**, its typical magnitude). It steps by their ratio:

```
m ← β1·m + (1 − β1)·g          β1 = 0.9
v ← β2·v + (1 − β2)·g²         β2 = 0.999 (0.95 for LLMs)
p ← p − lr · m̂ / (√v̂ + ε)      m̂, v̂: bias-corrected (below)
```

Dividing by √v normalises each parameter's step by its own gradient scale. In the valley, the steep coordinate has gradients 1,000× larger, and its step is divided by 1,000× more: both coordinates move at roughly `lr` per step. Adam is approximately invariant to how each parameter is scaled — and in a transformer, embedding rows, attention projections and LayerNorm gains differ in gradient scale by orders of magnitude.

**Bias correction.** m and v start at zero, so early on they are too small — v especially, because β2 = 0.999 averages over about 1,000 steps. Dividing by (1 − βᵗ) undoes that. Without it, the first update is (1 − β1)/√(1 − β2) ≈ **3.2× too large**, exactly when the model is least settled. With it, step one moves every parameter by exactly lr·sign(g) — lab 03's `adam-first-step` check.

---

_fig 1 — one Adam update for one parameter_

Components: g (gradient) · m ← β1·m + … (direction) · v ← β2·v + … (magnitude²) · bias-correct (÷ (1 − βᵗ)) · p −= lr·m̂/√v̂ (≈ lr per param)

Steps:
1. The gradient feeds two exponential moving averages: m tracks its direction over about 10 steps, v tracks its squared size over about 1,000.
2. Both averages start at zero and would be biased low for the first steps; dividing by 1 − βᵗ corrects them exactly.
3. The step is m̂/√v̂: a direction divided by a typical magnitude. A parameter with huge gradients and one with tiny gradients both move about lr per step.
4. The price is state: m and v are one fp32 number each per parameter — the 8 of T0.L4's 16 bytes per parameter, and the first thing ZeRO shards (D1.L3).

## AdamW: weight decay done right

**Weight decay** pulls every weight towards zero a little each step — a preference for small weights that helps generalisation (O1.L6). With SGD you can add `wd · p` to the gradient (an L2 penalty) and get exactly that. With Adam you cannot: the added term is divided by √v like everything else, so parameters with large gradients barely decay and parameters with tiny gradients decay at full speed. Loshchilov and Hutter's fix, **AdamW**, applies the decay directly to the weights, outside the adaptive step:

```
p ← p · (1 − lr · wd)          then the normal Adam update, with g untouched
```

Every LLM recipe you will read uses AdamW. In PyTorch, `torch.optim.Adam(weight_decay=…)` is the L2 version and `torch.optim.AdamW` the decoupled one — same argument name, different maths. Lab 03 implements both behind one flag.

---

> **[vendor snapshot — verified 2026-10]** A published LLM optimiser recipe
>
> Llama 2's pretraining used **AdamW with β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.95, ε = 10⁻⁵, weight decay 0.1**, gradient clipping at 1.0, 2,000 warmup steps and cosine decay to 10% of the peak learning rate. The lower β2 (0.95 instead of PyTorch's default 0.999) makes v react faster to sudden gradient growth, which helps stability in large runs (P1.L6). PyTorch's own AdamW defaults are β = (0.9, 0.999), ε = 10⁻⁸, weight decay 0.01.

**Q1. You switch from torch.optim.Adam(weight_decay=0.1) to torch.optim.AdamW(weight_decay=0.1) with everything else equal. What changed?**
   A. Nothing — AdamW is an alias
   B. Weight decay is now applied directly to the weights instead of being added to the gradient and rescaled by √v, so every parameter decays at the same rate
   C. AdamW turns off bias correction
   D. AdamW uses SGD for the decay and Adam for the gradient, doubling memory
   Answer: B — Adam's weight_decay is an L2 term added to g, which the adaptive denominator then rescales per parameter. AdamW applies p ← p·(1 − lr·wd) separately. Same argument name, different regulariser — a common source of irreproducible results when porting recipes.

**Q2. Why is SGD with a single learning rate slow on an ill-conditioned problem?**
   A. SGD has no momentum by default
   B. The learning rate must stay below 2 / (largest curvature) to be stable, which makes progress along the flattest direction proportional to smallest / largest curvature — tiny when the condition number is large
   C. SGD only works on convex problems
   D. The gradient is computed less accurately
   Answer: B — Stability is set by the steepest direction, speed by the flattest. On the bowl in this lesson that is a 0.19% improvement per step along x₁. Momentum and per-parameter scaling (Adam) both attack this ratio; a bigger learning rate just diverges.

**Q3. A 7B model is trained with AdamW. How much memory do Adam's m and v take in fp32?**
   A. 14 GB
   B. 28 GB
   C. 56 GB
   D. 112 GB
   Answer: C — Two fp32 numbers per parameter: 7 × 10⁹ × 8 bytes = 56 GB — half of T0.L4's 112 GB of training state, and more than the bf16 weights and gradients combined. That is why optimiser state is the first thing ZeRO shards and why 8-bit optimisers exist.

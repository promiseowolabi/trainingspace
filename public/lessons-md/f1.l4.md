# F1.L4 — Reward Models

_Track F1: Post-Training · ~17 min · trainingspace_

> People cannot write down a score for "a good answer", but they can say which of two answers is better. A reward model turns thousands of those comparisons into a function that scores any response — and a model optimised hard enough against it will find every flaw that function has.
SFT (F1.L2) needs demonstrations: someone must write the ideal response. That is expensive, and it caps quality at what the writer can produce. Judging is easier than writing — most people can tell which of two answers is better without being able to write the better one. **Preference data** exploits that: show a labeller a prompt and two responses, record which one they preferred.

## The Bradley–Terry model

To learn from comparisons, assume each response y to prompt x has a hidden scalar quality r(x, y), and that the probability a person prefers y₁ over y₂ is

```
P(y₁ ≻ y₂) = σ(r(x, y₁) − r(x, y₂))          σ = the logistic sigmoid
```

Only **differences** matter: adding a constant to every reward changes nothing. A gap of 0 is a coin flip; a gap of 1 is σ(1) = **73%**; a gap of 2 is **88%**; a gap of 4 is 98%.

Training a reward model r_θ is then maximum likelihood on the comparisons — which is binary cross-entropy on the margin:

```
loss = −log σ(r_θ(x, y_chosen) − r_θ(x, y_rejected))       averaged over pairs
```

Lab 13's `bradley_terry_loss` is this line. With equal rewards it is ln 2 = 0.693, the loss of knowing nothing.

---

- **50%** — gap 0 (σ(0): the reward model has no opinion)
- **73%** — gap 1 (σ(1) = 0.731)
- **88%** — gap 2 (σ(2) = 0.881)
- **ln 2** — initial loss (−log σ(0) = 0.693 nats per pair)

---

## Building one

A reward model is usually the SFT model itself with its vocabulary output layer replaced by a **single scalar head**: run the prompt and response through the transformer, read the final token's hidden state, project it to one number. Starting from a strong language model matters: judging a response requires understanding it.

Preference data is noisy. Labellers disagree with each other a substantial fraction of the time on close pairs, which caps the accuracy any reward model can reach on held-out comparisons — commonly around 65–75% for general chat data, higher on pairs with a clear winner. A reward model that scores 95% on its validation pairs is more likely to have learned a shortcut (such as "longer is better") than human taste.

_fig 1 — from comparisons to a scorer_

Components: prompt + 2 responses (human picks one) · reward model (LM + scalar head) · r(chosen) (scalar) · r(rejected) (scalar) · −log σ(Δr) (Bradley–Terry)

Steps:
1. Each training example is a prompt, two responses, and a label saying which one a person preferred.
2. The same reward model scores both responses independently: a language model whose last layer outputs one number instead of a vocabulary distribution.
3. The loss pushes the chosen score above the rejected one: −log σ of the difference, binary cross-entropy on the margin.
4. After training, the model scores any response to any prompt — which is what lets RL optimise against it without a human in the loop for every sample (F1.L5).

---

## Reward hacking

A reward model is a learned approximation of human preference, accurate near the kind of responses it was trained on. Optimise a policy hard against it and the policy drifts into responses the reward model has never seen, where its scores are wrong — and the optimiser seeks out precisely the places where they are wrong in its favour. Classic symptoms: responses get longer and longer, more confident, more flattering, full of formatting that labellers once liked — while their real quality stops improving or falls.

Gao et al. measured this directly with a "gold" reward model standing in for humans: as a policy was optimised against a smaller proxy reward model, the proxy score rose steadily while the gold score rose, peaked and then **declined**. The defences are the ones F1.L5 builds in: a KL penalty that keeps the policy close to its starting point, early stopping on held-out human evaluation, and refreshing the reward model with comparisons of the new policy's outputs.

**Q1. A reward model scores response A at 3.2 and response B at 1.2. Under Bradley–Terry, how likely is a labeller to prefer A?**
   A. about 62%
   B. about 73%
   C. about 88%
   D. about 96%
   Answer: C — Only the difference matters: Δ = 2.0, and σ(2) ≈ 0.881. The absolute values 3.2 and 1.2 carry no meaning on their own — adding 100 to both would predict the same preference.

**Q2. During RLHF, the reward model's scores keep rising while human ratings of samples have flattened and the responses have doubled in length. What is happening?**
   A. The policy is improving in ways humans cannot see
   B. Reward hacking: the policy has found responses the reward model over-scores (here, length), outside the distribution it was trained on — stop or strengthen the KL penalty, and retrain the reward model on the new outputs
   C. The learning rate is too low
   D. The human raters are wrong
   Answer: B — A proxy reward diverging from the real objective is the signature of over-optimisation. Length is the classic exploited feature because longer answers were often preferred in the training comparisons. The gold-versus-proxy experiments show exactly this shape.

**Q3. Your reward model reaches 97% accuracy on held-out preference pairs where labellers agree with each other about 70% of the time. What should you suspect first?**
   A. An excellent reward model
   B. A shortcut or leakage: no model should agree with noisy labels far more often than the labellers agree with each other — check for duplicate prompts across the split, or a feature such as length that predicts the label
   C. Too small a learning rate
   D. Too few epochs
   Answer: B — Inter-annotator agreement is a ceiling on meaningful accuracy. Exceeding it by a wide margin almost always means the evaluation is contaminated or the model has found a spurious cue — which will then be exploited during RL.

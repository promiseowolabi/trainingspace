# E1.L4 — Reading a Training Paper

_Track E1: Evals & Debugging · ~18 min · trainingspace_

> Every training paper is a set of numbers that must agree with each other: parameters, tokens, compute, hardware, hours. Put them in one table, recompute the ones you can, and you will know within ten minutes what the paper actually did — and where to look harder.
This course has given you every formula a training paper relies on. Reading one well means filling in a fixed table, and checking that its rows agree:

| row | where it comes from | check with |
|---|---|---|
| **N** — parameters | config table (layers, width, heads, vocabulary) | M1.L5's 12·L·C² + V·C |
| **D** — training tokens | data section | P1.L1 |
| **C** — compute | often not stated | 6·N·D (P1.L4) |
| tokens per parameter | D / N | P1.L5: 20 is compute-optimal; more is deliberate over-training |
| hardware and GPU-hours | infrastructure section, sometimes a carbon table | × peak FLOP/s → implied MFU |
| layout | TP × PP × DP, sharding, checkpointing | D1.L6; product = GPU count |
| optimiser recipe | AdamW β, ε, weight decay, peak LR, warmup, schedule, clipping, batch size in tokens | O1.L3, O1.L4, P1.L6 |
| precision | bf16, fp8, master weights | P1.L3 |
| data mixture | sources and weights, dedup, filtering, decontamination | P1.L1, E1.L1 |
| post-training | SFT size, preference data, method | F1 |
| evals | benchmarks, prompting, sample sizes | E1.L1 — compute the error bars yourself |

Lab 08's `budget.py` is the toolkit: `param_count`, `train_flops`, `mfu`, `training_days`, `chinchilla_optimal`.

## Worked example: Llama 2 7B

From the paper: 6.74 B parameters, 2.0 T training tokens, context 4,096, trained on A100-80GB GPUs for **184,320 GPU-hours** (its Table 2).

1. **Compute.** C = 6 × 6.74 × 10⁹ × 2.0 × 10¹² = **8.1 × 10²² FLOPs**.
2. **Achieved rate.** 184,320 GPU-hours × 3,600 s = 6.64 × 10⁸ GPU-seconds, so 8.1 × 10²² / 6.64 × 10⁸ = **1.22 × 10¹⁴ FLOP/s per GPU**.
3. **Implied MFU.** The A100's dense bf16 tensor-core peak is 312 TFLOP/s: 122 / 312 ≈ **39%** — squarely in the range well-tuned large runs report (P1.L4). The numbers agree.
4. **Tokens per parameter.** 2.0 T / 6.74 B ≈ **297** — about 15× Chinchilla's compute-optimal ratio: a deliberately over-trained small model, built to be cheap to serve (P1.L5).
5. **Counterfactual.** `chinchilla_optimal(8.1e22)` suggests about 26 B parameters on 520 B tokens for the same compute — a lower loss for the budget, and a model nearly 4× more expensive to run.

Five minutes of arithmetic, and the paper's headline figures are cross-checked against each other.

_fig 1 — cross-checking a paper_

Components: config (L, C, V → N) · data (D tokens) · C = 6·N·D (compute) · GPU-hours (× peak FLOP/s) · implied MFU (30–50%?)

Steps:
1. From the architecture table, compute the parameter count yourself and compare it with the stated size.
2. Multiply by the stated training tokens: six FLOPs per parameter per token gives the training compute, whether or not the paper states it.
3. From the reported GPU-hours and the hardware's peak, get the compute the hardware could have delivered.
4. Their ratio is the implied MFU. Around 30–50% is plausible for a large run; 90% or 5% means a number in the paper is not what it seems — and that is where to read carefully.

---

> **[warning]** - An implied MFU far outside 20–60% — a token count, a GPU-hour figure or the hardware is mis-stated, or compute was counted differently.
> - Benchmark gains smaller than the error bars of E1.L1, reported without intervals.
> - Comparisons with baselines evaluated under different prompts, few-shot settings or decoding.
> - Training data described too vaguely to judge contamination of the benchmarks being reported.
> - "We trained for N steps" with no batch size in tokens: steps are not a unit of training.

**Q1. A paper reports a 13B model trained on 1 T tokens in 20,000 H100-hours (989 TFLOP/s bf16 peak). What MFU does that imply, and how should you react?**
   A. About 39% — plausible
   B. About 110% — impossible: some figure (tokens, hours, parameters or the hardware) is wrong or counted differently; read the details before trusting other claims
   C. About 11% — low but plausible
   D. MFU cannot be computed from these numbers
   Answer: B — C = 6 × 13 × 10⁹ × 10¹² = 7.8 × 10²² FLOPs. 20,000 h × 3,600 s × 989 × 10¹² = 7.1 × 10²² FLOPs at 100% utilisation. The run would need about 110% MFU. A paper whose headline numbers contradict each other needs a closer read — perhaps the hours exclude something, or the token count includes repeats.

**Q2. A paper's model has 1,000 tokens per parameter. What does that tell you about its authors' goal?**
   A. They ran out of compute
   B. They deliberately over-trained a small model — paying extra training compute for a model that is cheaper to serve (P1.L5)
   C. They used a bad tokenizer
   D. The model is undertrained
   Answer: B — Compute-optimal training is about 20 tokens per parameter. 50× beyond that is a choice to minimise inference cost at a given quality, common for models meant to be widely deployed.

**Q3. Which single piece of information most often makes a paper's training run impossible to reproduce or sanity-check?**
   A. The model's name
   B. The global batch size in tokens and the total token count — without them, step counts, learning-rate schedules and compute cannot be related to anything
   C. The colour of the loss plot
   D. The number of authors
   Answer: B — Tokens are the unit everything else is measured in: compute (6·N·D), schedules (warmup and decay in tokens), and data repetition. A step count without a batch size in tokens cannot be converted into any of them.

# E1.L3 — Checkpointing and Resumption

_Track E1: Evals & Debugging · ~16 min · trainingspace_

> At cluster scale, interruptions are not exceptions — they are a rate. A long run is a sequence of resumes, and a resume that is not exact quietly changes the experiment. Know what state a step depends on, save all of it, and choose how often from the failure rate.
## What a step depends on

Lab 07's `resume-exact` check trains 20 steps straight and compares with 10 steps, a save, a load into a *differently initialised* model, and 10 more. Bit-for-bit equality requires every input of the next step to be restored:

| state | why | if you forget it |
|---|---|---|
| model weights | obviously | — |
| optimiser state (Adam's m, v, step count; fp32 master weights) | the update depends on them (O1.L3) | the first steps after resume behave like a fresh Adam: bias correction restarts, steps are mis-sized |
| scheduler position | the learning rate is a function of the step (O1.L4) | warmup reruns, or the cosine jumps |
| data position and sampler RNG | which batches come next | batches repeat or are skipped; the data mixture shifts |
| other RNG (dropout, sampling) | stochastic layers | not bit-exact, though usually harmless |
| gradient-accumulation and loss-scaler state | mid-step state, fp16 scaler (P1.L3) | a wrong first update |

The model weights are the smallest part. For a 7B model, a resumable checkpoint holds bf16 weights (14 GB) plus fp32 master weights, m and v (3 × 28 GB): about **98 GB**, seven times the inference checkpoint.

## How often to checkpoint

Checkpoint too rarely and each failure loses hours of compute; too often and the writes themselves eat the time. If writing a checkpoint stalls training for C and failures arrive on average every M, the classic approximation (Young, 1974) for the interval that minimises total waste is

```
interval ≈ √(2 · C · M)
```

With failures every 186 minutes (419 in 54 days) and a 2-minute checkpoint stall: √(2 × 2 × 186) ≈ **27 minutes**. Halve the stall and the optimal interval drops to about 19 minutes and the waste falls with it — which is why large runs invest in making checkpoints cheap:

- **Sharded checkpoints.** Each rank writes only its own shard of the state (D1.L3), in parallel, instead of gathering 98 GB onto one process.
- **Asynchronous checkpoints.** Copy the state to CPU memory (fast), resume training, and write to storage in the background.
- **In-memory redundancy.** Keep a recent copy in another node's RAM so a single-node failure restores from memory, not from storage.

---

_fig 1 — the cost of a failure_

Components: last checkpoint (step s) · work since (lost on failure) · failure (node dies) · restart + load (detect, reschedule, read) · recompute (steps since s)

Steps:
1. Every checkpoint costs a stall while state is captured — the C in √(2·C·M). Sharded, asynchronous checkpoints shrink it to seconds.
2. Every step after the last checkpoint is at risk. On average half an interval of work is lost per failure.
3. After a failure the job must be detected, rescheduled on healthy nodes and reloaded — minutes that automation and fast storage reduce.
4. Then the lost steps are recomputed. A resume that restores every piece of state makes them identical to the steps that were lost; anything less changes the run.

---

> **[segfault]** A resume that restores weights but not the data sampler starts reading the dataset from the beginning again. The loss curve looks perfectly continuous — the model has seen that data before, so it is if anything slightly lower — and the run quietly repeats its first tokens and never reaches the last ones. Lab 07's generator state is in the checkpoint for exactly this reason; at scale the equivalent is the data loader's position in each shard.

**Q1. A team resumes from a checkpoint that contains only the model's state_dict, with a freshly constructed AdamW. What goes wrong?**
   A. Nothing — the weights are what matters
   B. Adam's moments restart at zero and its step count at 1: bias correction makes the first updates about lr · sign(g) per parameter, a burst of large, poorly scaled steps that can cause a loss spike
   C. The learning rate becomes zero
   D. The model reverts to random initialisation
   Answer: B — m, v and t are part of the training state. A fresh Adam takes sign-like steps of size lr on every parameter (lab 03's adam-first-step) where the original run was taking carefully scaled ones — a common cause of a spike right after a restart.

**Q2. Failures arrive about every 8 hours and a checkpoint stalls training for 4 minutes. Roughly how often should you checkpoint?**
   A. every 4 minutes
   B. about every 62 minutes
   C. every 8 hours
   D. once a day
   Answer: B — √(2 × 4 × 480) ≈ 62 minutes. Checkpointing every few minutes would spend much of the time stalled; every 8 hours would lose about 4 hours of work per failure on average.

**Q3. Why is a resumable 7B checkpoint about 98 GB when the model is 14 GB?**
   A. Checkpoints are uncompressed
   B. It also stores the fp32 master weights and Adam's fp32 m and v — three more 28 GB tensors — which the next step needs
   C. It stores every previous step
   D. It stores the gradients and activations
   Answer: B — bf16 weights 14 GB + 3 × (7 × 10⁹ × 4 bytes) = 14 + 84 = 98 GB. Gradients and activations are recomputed and are not part of the checkpoint. The 14 GB file is the inference artefact; the 98 GB one is the training artefact.
